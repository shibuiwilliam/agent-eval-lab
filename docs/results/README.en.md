# How to Read These Results, and What They Add Up To

*日本語版: [README.md](README.md)*

A record of implementing Chapter 3 (PTS), Chapter 4 (process and plan evaluation) and Chapter 8 (countermeasures against test obsolescence) of the report *Overlooked Evaluation Methods for AI Agents* on top of a small home-grown agent and environment, and confirming numerically that each method **reacts to a planted defect and stays quiet on the control**.

The verdict list is in [summary.en.md](summary.en.md) (generated by `uv run agenteval verify`).
Each experiment's five items, method, numbers and verdict live in `E*.en.md`.
**On 2026-09-19 the code and experiment design were reviewed end to end, 8 defects in the evaluator
were fixed, and all 27 experiments were re-run.** That record is in [`REVIEW.en.md`](../REVIEW.en.md).
**On 2026-09-20/21 chapter 3 (PTS) was rebuilt and three experiments were added, bringing the total to 30.**
Training data was created from 65 synthetic changes (report 3.8's cold start), and
**change history and test history are now recorded as a ledger so that lag and churn become features**.
That moved the escape-defect rate from 0.868× random to **0.319×**, and regression recall at a
30% budget from 0.535 to **0.819**. The record is in [`PTS.en.md`](../PTS.en.md).

## Read this first (provenance)

**The numbers in 28 of the experiments have provenance `simulated` (E8-8 alone is `unit`); only E0-1 and E0-2 carry `live`.**
Trajectories are produced by a deterministic simulated agent (`src/agenteval/llm/simulator.py`, ADR-008).

However, **the validity of sim itself was verified twice against live (the real API)**
→ [LIVE.en.md](LIVE.en.md) (round 1), [LIVE2.en.md](LIVE2.en.md) (round 2), [E0-1](E0-1.en.md), [E0-2](E0-2.en.md).
That verification found defects in sim, and all experiments were re-taken after fixing them (details in [`IMPROVEMENT.en.md`](../IMPROVEMENT.en.md)). The essentials:

- Quantities where sim **agrees** with live: outcomes (agreement 0.82), step count, verification rate,
  duplicate-call rate, **stop appropriateness** (live 0.496 / sim 0.500), **premature completion**
  (live 0.471 / sim 0.450)
- Quantities where sim **disagrees** with live: `finish_called` (live 0.683 / sim 1.000).
  A real agent sometimes finishes with prose instead of calling `finish`.
  **Metrics that depend on the `finish` call (claims, self-report fidelity) must not be discussed using sim numbers**
- **Correction**: earlier versions of this page said "sim cannot reproduce stop appropriateness
  (sim 1.0 / live 0.66)". That was a **measurement error**. Measured per report 4.2 (declaring
  completion while unmet; continuing after achieving), live and sim agree. The pre-revision
  implementation only checked whether the last tool call was `finish` (ADR-019)
- Before live verification, sim had the effect of the planted version v07 **backwards** (sim 0.30 / live 0.97)
- The planted defect in v02_dateformat **was not reproducing on the real model** (when the system prompt and a tool definition conflict, the real model prefers the tool definition). The prompt was rebuilt and reproduction confirmed live
- The deterministic surrogate judge **does not agree** with the live Sonnet 5 judge (within ±1: 0.683; exact: 0.35).
  The surrogate is consistently too lenient. The **absolute score level** of judge-based experiments cannot be discussed in sim ([E0-2](E0-2.en.md))
- **Sim is harsher than the real model.** Its pass-rate level sits 0.18 below live (E0-1's `pass_rate_gap`)

The simulated agent is built so that it
- reads its behaviour from the **section markers in the version's system prompt** (standing in for the path by which an LLM follows a prompt),
- takes the task's intent from `Task.sim` (a field written independently of the acceptance criteria), and
- draws its errors from a "luck" vector fixed by `(task_id, seed, repeat)` (the same luck is reused across versions, making comparisons paired).

Consequently, **some experiments can be circular: a property deliberately embedded in the simulator's policy is then detected by the metric that measures that property.** Each result page states individually, under "findings and limitations", which parts are circular and which are not. The degree of circularity differs per experiment:

| Circularity | Examples | How to read it |
|---|---|---|
| Strong (structurally inevitable) | E4-1 (v04's policy repeats searches), E8-6 (probe responses from an artificially altered distribution), E8-9's gap | Shows only that the method's implementation runs |
| Moderate | E8-4 (the drop in κ), E8-5 (factor attribution) | The mechanism is real (renaming an external service's key really does change outcomes), but the magnitude is a matter of configuration |
| Weak (the result is not foreseeable) | E3-1 / E3-3 / E3-7 (selection escape-defect rates), E3-5 (savings rate), E3-6 (SPRT trial count), E8-3 (discriminative power), E4-2 (wasted-call rate) | The properties of the implemented method show up in the numbers. The negative results came from here |

## Negative results (NEGATIVE): 6

### E3-3 Uncertainty-driven selection cannot beat random — but its calibration holds

This is the result after the review fixed two defects ([`REVIEW.en.md`](../REVIEW.en.md)):
(1) the escape-defect rate's `F_full` was restored to the report's "failures found by a full run"
(ADR-018), and (2) the controls now include the inviolable set (ADR-020).

**What held**: with `expected_escape` and the measurement on the same quantity, the calibration
error is **0.080** (criterion 0.15). The selector correctly estimates how many failures it leaves
behind. The pre-revision 0.325 was an artefact of subtracting a prediction about failures from a
measurement about flips.

**What did not hold**: the escape-defect rate is 0.778 against random's 0.737 — **worse** (ratio
1.056, criterion 0.5). On the flip-based KPI the ratio is 1.137, again worse. The attainable floor
within budget (an oracle) is 0.155, so the criterion is not unattainable.

Five policies, as ratios against random (below 1 is better than random):

| Policy | Failure-KPI ratio | Flip-KPI ratio |
|---|---|---|
| The report's pipeline (coverage-first + redundancy) | 1.056 | 1.137 |
| Redundancy penalty removed | 0.948 | 0.622 |
| `H(p̂)/c` alone | 1.010 | 0.973 |
| Failure probability `(1−p̂)/c` | 0.760 | 0.797 |
| **Coverage overlap alone** | 0.839 | **0.344** |

**Implication**: in this environment the signal lives in report 3.2 (coverage), not 3.4
(uncertainty). It is theoretically coherent: Escape is minimised by selecting the tests most likely
to fail, but `H(p̂)` peaks at `p̂ = 0.5` and **deprioritises the tests that almost certainly fail**.
**Maximising information gain (3.4's objective) and minimising the escape-defect rate (3.8's KPI)
are different optimisation problems; optimising one does not optimise the other.** The report
presents them together as "the selector's KPI".

### E3-5 The savings from branch re-execution did not materialise

Agreement on `outcome.passed` was 1.0 (60 pairs) and the self-branch used 0 billed steps, so **the
correctness of the method held**. What did not hold was the savings: v02's live step ratio is 1.000
(the same as a normal run). Fixing the double-billing brought it down from 1.113, but the criterion
is 0.6 (ADR-021). The cause is structural: retracing a prefix costs one "decision confirmation" call
per step, and once the version changes that is always billed. The earlier the divergence, the more
the confirmations cost. The token ratio is 1.024, lower than the step ratio, so the prefix cache
showed up only on the token side.
**Implication**: report 3.6 says "execution cost falls from trajectory length L to suffix length
L − i*", but **that confirmation cost is not in the model**. The savings should be discussed in
token terms rather than step counts, and even then only for changes that diverge late.

### E3-7 The skeleton of the method holds; the escape-defect rate does not

The inviolable set is selected 100% of the time even at a 5% budget, ε-exploration mixes in 2 tests
every time, and a 100% budget gives an escape-defect rate of 0. **The skeleton holds.** What did not
hold is the selection-quality claim "escape-defect rate ≤ 0.1 at a 40% budget", measured at 0.479.
An oracle that knows each repeat's `F_full` reaches 0.031 at the same budget, so **the criterion is
not unattainable — the selection is what falls short.** Same cause as E3-3.

### E8-3 Outcome-only discriminative power cannot discriminate process degradation

The saturation judgement on trivial tasks (|d| = 0, saturated = true) and the notification to the
lifecycle held. What did not hold was discriminative power on boundary tasks: 0.068 against 0.3.
The cause is that among the bad-labelled versions, v03_noverify (declaring completion without
checking) and v04_loopy (duplicate calls) **do not lower the pass rate at all**. Since `d(t)`
separates good from bad using outcomes alone, it cannot in principle discriminate a version whose
output is the same and whose process has degraded.
**Implication**: the discriminative power of 8.4 needs to be defined over process metrics (linter
violation rate, verification rate) as well. This is the mirror image, from the test-quality side, of
the claim in 4.1 that outcome and process are independent axes.

### E3-9 Whether exploration keeps the selector calibrated cannot be decided at this scale

Report 3.8 gives ε-exploration the role of "keeping the selector calibrated". Measured by processing
37 changes sequentially under partial feedback. **The first implementation tried only one ordering,
and on it the calibration gain read +0.006.** With five orderings and a paired comparison it becomes
**−0.0023 (paired SD 0.0088)** — the effect disappeared. A power calculation says **113 orderings**
are needed to resolve an effect of this size (407 for the escape-defect rate).
**Implication**: this did not show that exploration has no effect; it showed that **any effect is
smaller than five orderings can resolve**. The cost of exploring did stay within the criterion
(0.049 against a gain of 0.058), refuting the worry that exploration sacrifices much current-round
performance. As a side observation, **full observation's late Brier (0.088) is worse than partial
observation at ε=0 (0.068)** — the control intended as a ceiling did not act as one.
See [PTS.en.md](../PTS.en.md).

### E0-2 The deterministic surrogate judge does not agree with the live judge

The second half of the hypothesis held strongly: the live Sonnet 5 judge is stable (re-judge
agreement 1.000 within ±1, 0.967 exact) and degrades mutated steps correctly (0.989). What did not
hold was the first half — agreement with the surrogate is 0.683 within ±1 (criterion 0.7) and only
0.35 exact. The disagreement is one-directional: **the surrogate is consistently too lenient** (on
wrong-tool mutations, live scores 1.73 against the surrogate's 4.00). `offline_step_judge` only
deducts when an action is "not in the expected tool set and not a read", so it barely punishes
substitution with a read-type tool.
**Implication**: the direction of E4-5's conclusion survives under live, but the **absolute score
level** from a surrogate judge cannot be trusted.

## Limitations of this project

1. **The individual experiments were not re-taken under live.** Live verification went as far as
   sim's validity (E0-1) and the judge (E0-2); the other 28 experiments remain sim. The parts that
   depend on an LLM's semantic understanding are replaced by deterministic stand-ins, and what they
   demonstrated is that the plumbing connects.
2. **This is an illustrative environment**: SQLite, 14 tools, 46 tasks (ADR-003).
3. **Production traffic is generated** (ADR-006).
4. **Re-judging after a judge changeover is not verified.** E0-2 measured live-judge stability, but
   not what happens when the judge model is swapped.
5. **The task set is homogeneous.** With only 13 distinct tool-name sequences, similarity-based
   redundancy judgement does not work. The E3-3 ablation shows removing redundancy moves the
   flip KPI from 1.137 to 0.622.
6. **In some experiments the simulator's policy determines the result.** See the circularity table.
7. **PTS presupposes that change history and test history are recorded.** Without history, learning
   loses to the dependency graph; with a ledger plus lag and churn, the escape-defect rate drops to
   0.319× random. But 65 changes and 122 positives sit orders of magnitude below industrial PTS,
   leaving too little history to delegate feature-group selection ([PTS.en.md](../PTS.en.md)).
8. **Defects in the evaluator only surface by reading the code.** Live verification found 3; the
   2026-09-19 review found 8; the 2026-09-20/21 PTS rebuild found 5 more. None was visible from
   "do the experiments run and pass?".

## If there were a next step

1. **Re-confirm under live, restricted to the negative results.** Priority: E3-3 / E3-7 (can
   selection beat random?) > E3-5 (savings) > E8-3 (discriminative power). From E0-1's measurement
   (about 0.007 USD per run), a live corpus at the 46-task scale is about 25 USD.
2. **Diversify the tasks' tool sequences.** The E3-3 ablation makes it clear that the redundancy
   penalty is destroying signal, and per ADR-014 the cause is sim's trajectory monotony rather than
   the similarity definition.
3. **Define discriminative power over process metrics, `d_process(t)`** (the implication of E8-3).
4. **Align the selector's objective with the KPI** (the implication of E3-3). If the goal is to
   lower the escape-defect rate, order by failure probability `(1−p̂)` rather than `H(p̂)`, and
   record the mismatch between report 3.4 and 3.8.
5. **Build multi-stage niah tasks** (E4-7), long enough for the comparison of summarising strategies
   to hold.
